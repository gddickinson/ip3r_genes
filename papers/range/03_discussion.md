## Discussion

The IP₃ receptor is an ancestral eukaryotic gene. It is present across the
animals, in amoebozoans, excavates, ciliates, oomycetes, dinoflagellates,
haptophytes and green algae, and in early-diverging fungi, and it is absent
from every prokaryote swept. That distribution cannot be explained by a
single origin inside the animals, and the ryanodine receptor in a
choanoflagellate places the split between the two families before Metazoa
[R35, R37]. The absences are then losses, and they are independent. Land
plants lost the receptor after diverging from the green algae that still
carry it [R202], and the fungi lost it along more than one branch: Dikarya,
Glomeromycota, Mortierellomycota, Kickxellomycota and Microsporidia are all
empty while Mucoromycota and Chytridiomycota are not, a pattern read against
the accepted fungal backbone [R212] as several losses rather than one. Loss
of this kind is an ordinary evolutionary mechanism [R193], and several of the
empty lineages are ones with reduced genomes or reorganised calcium
signalling: the apicomplexans and microsporidia [R201, R36], the diatoms and
red algae [R199], and the tapeworms, which have shed gene families across
their biology [R200].

For the land plants the result puts a genomic answer under a question the
physiological literature has kept open [R196]. Plant cells respond to IP₃,
and no IP₃ receptor gene exists in any of 384 land-plant proteomes or 25
controlled land-plant assemblies. Whatever mediates that response is built
from other channel families [R197, R198]. We cannot say what it is; we can
say that it is not a divergent member of this family missed by a
domain-based search, because the same search finds the family in the green
algae, finds the MIR domain hundreds of times in land plants, and was made
at a sensitivity loose enough to recover a single IP₃-binding domain.

Three design decisions carry the result, and each is visible in the data
rather than asserted. The family call is positive at every stage, so a
record is an IP₃ receptor because it is closer to IP₃ receptors than to
ryanodine receptors and not because it failed to look like the sister family;
on the first benchmark run the obvious instrument called every ryanodine
receptor an IP₃ receptor. The census counts proteomes and genomes rather than
records, so a well-sequenced lineage cannot outvote a sparse one. And every
absence at genome level carries a control chosen for its own clade by
measurement, which is what turned a fixed control that fired once per
apicomplexan class into one that fires in every apicomplexan proteome.

**The claims have limits, and they are stated with the numbers.** A
proteome absence covers the gene sets of 6,928 reference proteomes and a
genome absence covers 194 assemblies; a lineage not sampled is not covered.
The profiles are labelled by seeds, and the deep branches rest on seven
non-metazoan seeds, so a lineage more diverged than any of them is outside
what the profile call has been shown to reach. The labelled-bait margin
cannot reach one characterised receptor, *Dictyostelium* iplA, which is in
the profile seed set for that reason. No green-plant gene span could be
measured, so land-plant assemblies are judged against the global median
span. The one other full-length ryanodine receptor outside the animals, in
*Capsaspora owczarzaki*, is itself a profile seed and is not independent
evidence. The fold test confirms the family call where cryo-EM structures
exist and does not reach the deep lineages, whose only models are partial and
of low confidence. Finally, the vertebrate absences are not addressed here:
the vertebrate proteomes that carry no receptor record were leads for a genome
sweep, not losses, and whether any vertebrate has lost a paralogue is a
separate question with its own controls.

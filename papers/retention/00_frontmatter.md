## Abstract

Vertebrates carry three inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor genes,
*ITPR1*, *ITPR2* and *ITPR3*, and gene families of that age normally lose
copies in some lineages. Whether any vertebrate has lost one of the three
has never been asked of the genomes themselves, and a survey that reports no
loss is only as good as its measured ability to find a gene that is there.
We aligned a 38-protein bait panel, with the ryanodine receptors as an
internal positive control, to a declared scope of 309 vertebrate assemblies
and recorded 1,236 genome × cell results. The control fired in every genome.
We then restated all 927 genome × paralogue cells under eight ordered
positive tests, of which exactly one licenses a loss. No cell reaches it,
and Dollo parsimony places zero losses on the family character and on each
paralogue. We measured the search's own miss rate on the same data: because
no loss is reconstructed, every cell whose gene is independently known to be
present and was not found is a false negative, 140 of 923 cells overall,
falling to 5 of 563 in assemblies whose contigs are longer than the gene,
with an independent ryanodine receptor series agreeing. An exact ablation of
the bait panel shows that paralogue coverage, not phylogenetic breadth, sets
recall. Finally, we name what it would take to manufacture a loss: the
family-level coding yields one in 2 of 32 analytical settings, the
paralogue-resolved coding in 18 of 32. Every vertebrate genome able to hold
the gene carries all three paralogues.
